Legal information

Your legal options, in plain words

The law can offer real protection, and you don't need to know it all to start. This page explains the basics. Laws differ by state, so use the links below for your state's rules, and talk to a legal aid office or advocate about your situation.

This is general information, not legal advice. For advice about your case, contact a lawyer, a free legal aid organization, or a trained advocate.

Protective orders

Protective and restraining orders

Available to people of any gender. The name varies by state: protective order, restraining order, order of protection.

What one can do

A court order that can forbid the other person from contacting you, coming near you, or harming or harassing you, your children, or others close to you. Depending on your state, it may also cover things like temporary custody or pets.

How it usually works

You apply at your local courthouse. If you're in danger, you can often get a temporary order the same day, without the other person there. A full hearing follows, usually within a few weeks, where both sides can speak.

Cost, and whether you need a lawyer

In many states it's free, and you don't need a lawyer to apply, though legal aid or an advocate can help you prepare. Ask the court clerk or check your state's page on WomensLaw.

What it can't do

An order only works if the other person obeys it and police enforce it. It doesn't stop every kind of abuse, and it doesn't replace a safety plan. Keep a copy with you, and share it with your employer and your children's school.

If you report to police

Being prepared

Reporting is your choice. If you decide to, these things can help.

Bring what you have

  • Photos of injuries or damage
  • Messages, voicemails, your dated notes
  • Any existing court orders
  • A supportive person, especially a witness

Tell them the important things

  • Whether they have guns or have used weapons
  • Any choking or strangulation, even once
  • Past orders, warrants, or aliases
  • Ask to be interviewed apart from them

Before you leave

  • The officer's name and badge number
  • The report number
  • Contact details for victim services or an advocate

Other situations

Housing, children, and immigration

Housing

If you live in public or HUD-assisted housing, federal law (VAWA) says you can't be denied housing or evicted because you experienced abuse. You can ask to have the abuser removed from the lease, or ask for an emergency transfer to another unit. Many states also let survivors end a private lease early.

HUD: VAWA housing rights

Custody and your children

Family court can consider abuse when deciding custody and visits, and you can ask for safety measures like supervised visits or safe handoffs. This area is complex; if you can, get help from legal aid or a family-law attorney.

Immigration

If your spouse or parent is a US citizen or permanent resident and abused you, you may be able to apply for status yourself under VAWA, without them knowing. Other protections exist for crime victims. See an immigration attorney or accredited legal aid, not a notary.
