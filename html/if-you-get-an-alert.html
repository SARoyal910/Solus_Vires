For trusted contacts

If you get a check-in alert

Someone chose you as the person to check on them if they missed a scheduled check-in. An alert doesn't mean something is definitely wrong. Phones die and people forget. But they trusted you with this, so it's worth acting on.

If you believe they're in danger right now, or you hear or see anything alarming when you try to reach them, call 911 and give their address if you know it.

What to do

Four steps

1. Reach out the way they'd expect

Call or text them like you normally would. If you agreed on a code word or a safe way to contact them, use it. Keep the message ordinary; someone else may see their phone.

2. Don't contact the person hurting them

Don't call, message, or confront their partner or the person they're afraid of, and don't mention the alert to them. It could put your friend in more danger.

3. If you can't reach them

Try again, and try anyone they told you is safe to ask. If you're still worried, you can ask police for a "welfare check," or call 911 if you believe they're in danger.

4. Talk to an advocate

Not sure what to do? Hotline advocates help friends and family too, 24/7: 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788.

Keep it private

Don't post, don't forward

Don't share the alert or post about it, even to ask if anyone has seen them. Their location and situation may need to stay private for their safety.

How the alerts work

What you'll receive

You'll get an alert by email, and by notification on any device you turned them on for. If they still haven't checked in, the alert repeats every 6 hours, numbered so you can tell a repeat from the first one ("Check-in alert 2"). When they check in, or turn check-ins off, you'll get one more message saying the alerts have stopped.

That message only means someone signed in to their account. If you were already worried, it's still fine to reach out the way you normally would.

You can stop receiving alerts at any time using the "manage" link in any alert email.
