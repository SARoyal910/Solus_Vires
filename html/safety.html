Safety planning

Make a plan that fits your life

A safety plan is a personal, practical plan for staying safer: while you're living with someone who hurts you, while you get ready to leave, and after you've left. You don't have to be planning to leave to make one. Take what fits and skip what doesn't. You know your situation best.

You don't have to do this alone. A Hotline advocate can build a plan with you, 24/7: 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788. The Hotline also has an interactive safety plan tool.
Leaving is often the most dangerous time. If you can, plan it with an advocate, and think about whether someone could see this page on this device. Quick Exit leaves this site, but it can't erase your browser history.

While you're living with them

Staying safer right now

Know your safer spots

Think about which rooms have a way out and no weapons. If an argument starts, try to move there early. Rooms with knives or tools, like kitchens and garages, and small rooms with one way out, like bathrooms, are riskier.

Pick a code word

Agree on a word or text with someone you trust that means "I need help, call the police." Make sure they know exactly what to do when they get it.

Keep help within reach

Keep your phone charged and nearby if you can, and memorize a few numbers, or keep them on paper, in case your phone is taken.

Practice getting out

Know how you'd leave in a hurry: which door, where your keys are, where you'd go. If you drive, park facing out and keep fuel in the tank.

Tell someone your plan

Share your plan and your usual schedule with one trusted person, so they know when to worry and what to do.

Write it down, safely

A dated record of what happens, with photos and messages, can help later with protective orders or custody. Keep it where they can't find it.

If you're thinking about leaving

Getting ready

There's no right timeline. Small steps now can make a big difference later.

Know where you'll go

A friend's place, family, or a shelter. Shelters can explain their rules and whether they have space before you arrive. 211 or the Hotline can help you find one.

Set money aside

Even small amounts help. Consider keeping cash, or an account they don't know about, with someone you trust.

Pack a go-bag

Keep it somewhere they won't look, ideally with a trusted friend who isn't a mutual friend or next-door neighbor, since those are easier for them to guess.

What to take

Documents and essentials

Copies are better than nothing. Photos of documents, stored somewhere safe, count too.

For you and your children

  • Driver's license or ID, passports, birth certificates
  • Social Security cards
  • Green cards, visas, and other immigration papers
  • Health insurance cards and medical records
  • Marriage license, lease or deed
  • Any court orders: protective orders, custody orders
  • Children's school and medical records

Everyday essentials

  • Medications and prescriptions
  • Cash, bank cards, checkbook
  • Keys: house, car, work
  • Phone and charger
  • A change of clothes
  • A list of important numbers: friends, family, doctors, school, your local domestic violence program

Information that may help in court

  • The other person's date of birth and license plate number
  • Pay stubs and bank account details
  • A list of credit cards, yours and shared

Evidence

  • Photos of injuries or damage
  • Threatening texts, emails, or voicemails
  • Your dated notes about what happened

Ask an advocate or lawyer before sharing evidence with anyone.

Technology

Phones, accounts, and being tracked

Use a safer device

If you think your phone or computer is monitored, look for help from a device they've never had access to, like a library computer or a friend's phone.

Check shared accounts

Shared phone plans, cloud accounts, and family-sharing settings can reveal your location, calls, and photos. Review location sharing on your phone and apps.

Change things carefully

Sudden changes, like new passwords or removing an app, can alert the person watching. It's often safer to make those changes as part of a plan, with an advocate.

If you have children

Keeping kids safer

A safe place and a plan

Choose a room or a neighbor's home they can go to when they're scared. Teach them how and when to call 911 and who else they can call.

A code word

Agree on a word that means "it's time to leave." Make sure they know not to tell anyone what it means.

Stay out of it

Tell them clearly that it's not their job to stop the violence, even to protect you. Their job is to get safe.

Safer handoffs

If you share custody, exchange children somewhere public and busy, bring someone with you, or arrange pickups through school.

School and childcare

Tell them who is and isn't allowed to pick up your children, and give them a copy of any court order.

Pets

Planning for your animals

Many people stay because they're afraid for their pets. You can plan for them too.

Put them in your name

Register licenses, vaccinations, and microchips in your name only, and make sure the other person isn't the emergency contact on a microchip.

Find somewhere for them

Ask trusted friends, local animal shelters, and domestic violence programs about temporary care. Some shelters accept pets.

Hotline: pet safety

Take their things

Food, medications, vet records, and your vet's number. In some states, a protective order can include your pets.

After you've left

Staying safer afterward

Change what they know

If you can, change your locks and phone number. Vary your routines: a different route to work or school, a different store or bank.

Tell the right people

Let trusted friends, neighbors, and your employer know what to look out for. If you have a protective order, carry a copy and share it with them.

Look after yourself

Leaving is hard, and it's normal to feel grief, fear, or doubt. You don't have to have it figured out.

Specific situations

If this applies to you

Immigration status

If your partner or parent is a US citizen or permanent resident and abused you, federal law (VAWA) lets you apply for status yourself, without them knowing or agreeing. It applies to people of any gender. Other options exist for crime victims. Talk to an immigration attorney or legal aid, not a notary.

USCIS: VAWA self-petitions

If they're also your caregiver

Keep your medications and mobility or breathing devices under your own control. Ask to speak with doctors privately. Find backup help from friends, family, or Adult Protective Services, and check that a shelter is accessible before you go.

Hotline: caregiver abuse
